A pair is one piece of the puzzle. It measures a repeating spatial pattern, not a complete picture. Several sky positions can give the same response. Different baseline lengths and directions help remove this ambiguity.
τ = −B · s / c   ·   Δφ = 2πfτ   ·   fringe spacing ≈ λ/B
The dot product selects the baseline component along the source direction. Signals here are illustrative sinusoids; actual astronomical radio signals are noise-like and are cross-correlated. The sky map shows the real part of the complex fringe response; an interferometer measures both components. Compensation tracks the selected source, shifting the fringes so it sits at a maximum.

The synthesised beam

The central feature sets the resolution; surrounding ripples create false features around real sources. Better coverage usually reduces these artefacts. This small map spans the same 30′ field.

θ ≈ λ / Bmax, projected   ·   pairs = N(N − 1)/2
Each baseline samples the Fourier transform of the sky brightness. This simulation evaluates Gaussian-source visibilities, grids them on a 512 × 512 Fourier plane with natural weighting and applies an inverse Fourier transform. The raw result is a dirty image: no CLEAN deconvolution is applied. Negative bowls and sidelobes are expected. Earth rotation changes projected baseline lengths and directions, filling tracks rather than making a filled dish.

Geometry: ideal horizontal array at the geographic North Pole, observing the selected declination. Duration runs symmetrically around transit. This keeps the projection exact while avoiding hidden below-horizon observations; declination 0° is the horizon limit. Small-field, narrow-band approximation; primary-beam attenuation, antenna shadowing, atmosphere and calibration errors are omitted. Noise is illustrative complex visibility noise, not an instrument sensitivity prediction. A larger baseline does not give the collecting area of a filled dish of that diameter.
Tip: compare a snapshot and a 12-hour observation without changing the layout. Longer observations add directions as Earth turns; they do not physically move the dishes.

Model notes & sources

Resolution and sensitivity are different. Map colours are normalised for visibility, so brightness cannot be compared as an absolute flux between panels. Target morphology is held fixed with wavelength to isolate instrumental effects. The single-dish field is 120′ wide; the two-dish and array fields are 30′ wide. Changing stages changes field size, not the physical meaning of an arcminute.
