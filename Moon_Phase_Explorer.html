Using the explorer & model assumptions

In the orbital view, drag the Moon along the outer ring to change its position relative to the Sun. Drag the mint observer marker around Earth to turn your local meridian towards or away from the Sun. You can also click either ring, drag across the lunar disc, or use the sliders with the arrow keys.

In Explore independently mode, Play cycle holds the time of day fixed through a 29.53-day phase cycle. Play day turns Earth while holding the Moon at the chosen phase. The angle at Earth is the Sun–Moon separation. At first quarter it is 90° east of the Sun, and half the disc is illuminated. The bright limb always faces the Sun in the local sky.

Linked time advances Earth’s rotation and the Moon’s orbital position together. One simulated solar day turns Earth through 360° relative to the Sun and advances the Moon by about 12.2° around the phase cycle. The Sun stays fixed in the orbital diagram, so lunar motion there is measured relative to the Sun. One phase cycle takes 29.53 simulated days.

In Linked time, dragging either object or moving either slider scrubs time for all three views. Dragging across midnight or new Moon continues smoothly. Phase presets choose a new starting phase at the current clock time and restart the elapsed-time counter. Changing mode pauses playback and starts a new counter from the current scene. Reset restores first quarter at 18:00 in Red Row, Sky upright and the default speed within the selected mode. The speed slider sets simulated minutes or hours per real second.

The panorama runs from north through east, south and west, back to north. It uses your chosen latitude and longitude, local solar time, and a flat horizon. Red Row defaults to approximately 55.2863° N, 1.6039° W. Changing longitude preserves the simulated instant and shifts local solar time. Greenwich solar time provides a common time reference; this teaching clock ignores time zones, daylight saving and the equation of time. In this teaching model both bodies follow the celestial equator, as the Sun does at the equinox. The orbit is circular, symbols are enlarged, and the lunar surface is a photographic map projected onto a sphere with simplified illumination. The model omits orbital inclination, libration, terrain shadows, eclipses, atmospheric refraction and seasonal changes. Moon upright keeps lunar north at the top. Sky upright rotates the disc relative to the local vertical, treating lunar north as celestial north in this simplified geometry. At the zenith and nadir, azimuth and sky orientation are undefined, so the panorama uses a fixed direction for that instant. At either pole, longitude sets the reference meridian and the equatorial tracks follow the horizon. Use it to explore geometry rather than predict dated observations.
