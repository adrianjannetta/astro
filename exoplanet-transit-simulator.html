How the model works · equations, assumptions & source

Reading the evidence

The stellar disc uses a fixed pixels-per-solar-radius scale, so its size changes with stellar mass. The dashed circle marks a star of radius 1 R☉. The planet shares the same physical scale.

A plain transit gives the planet’s size relative to its star. For a small planet crossing a uniformly bright star away from the edge:

fractional depth ≈ (Rp / R★)²

Double Rp and the depth becomes four times larger. The time between repeated transits gives the orbital period.

The duration runs from the solid planet’s first contact to its last contact. In the dust-tail example, the dust can keep dimming the star after the solid core has left. A grazing transit clips the edge of the star; it need not reach the depth predicted by the simple formula.

What is calculated?

The orbit follows Kepler’s equation. The period includes the masses of both bodies:

P² = 4π²a³ / G(M★ + Mp)

Here a is the relative orbit’s semi-major axis. The brightness uses the overlapping areas of the two discs. Optional limb darkening is integrated over 80 concentric rings.

Inclination is 90° for an edge-on orbit and 0° for a face-on orbit. The alignment readout uses the minimum projected separation b, in stellar radii.

Scope & provenance

Rebuilt from the supplied University of Nebraska–Lincoln Flash Exoplanet Transit Simulator, with its main controls, stellar mass relations and historical example parameters.

Legacy presets are teaching examples from the old file, not current measurements. Orientation is expressed here as the conventional periastron angle ω. This is a new implementation, not a pixel-for-pixel port.

The star is spherical and on the main sequence. The planet is dark and spherical. Atmospheres, reflected light, secondary eclipses and other planets are omitted from the plain model. Orbits intersecting the star are flagged as invalid.

The two extra examples are illustrative models inspired by observed systems, not fits to particular observations. HAT-P-11 b uses approximate historical planetary parameters and one stationary circular spot in projection. Spot contrast and placement are chosen for teaching. Its light is normalized to the spotted star outside transit.

The Kepler-1520 b example has an illustrative core and an exponentially fading tail represented by 90 partly transparent dust parcels. Absorption is added in an optically thin approximation; stellar intensity at each parcel’s centre is used if limb darkening is enabled. Dust overlaps and occultation by the small core are not corrected. The diagram and curve use the same parcel locations and opacity weights; amber is a visibility aid. Dust dynamics, forward-scattering brightening and transit-to-transit changes are not included.

Scientific background: Béky et al. (2014), spot-crossing model; Bakos et al., HAT-P-11 b; Rappaport et al. (2012), disintegrating planet; Budaj (2013), dust-tail light curve.
