Teaching notes, model assumptions & data

Angles at different places

Elongation ε is the Sun–Earth–planet angle. Phase angle α is the Sun–planet–Earth angle. The illuminated fraction is (1 + cos α) / 2. A 90° elongation is quadrature; it does not imply a half-lit planet.

Inferior and superior refer to orbits inside and outside Earth’s orbit. Inferior planets show the full range of phases. Superior planets remain gibbous or full. “Superior conjunction” can also name the far-side conjunction of an inferior planet.

Retrograde motion

The new panel measures geocentric ecliptic longitude against fixed stars: positive rate is direct (eastward), negative rate is retrograde (westward). S1 and S2 mark stationary longitude, not a pause in heliocentric orbital motion. Try Mars in both modes. The sky map retains equal angular scales; the longitude–time graph shows the reversal even when the track overlaps itself. The future path is a calculated preview, not an observation. See NASA’s explanation of apparent planetary motion.

Simple and realistic models

In Simple mode, orbits are circular, coplanar and prograde, viewed from the north. The default scene is a teaching configuration, not today’s sky. Orbital distances share one linear scale; physical discs are enlarged. The view refits when the selected orbit radius changes, but does not rescale during an animation.

Realistic mode uses fixed eccentric, inclined Keplerian orbits with the Sun at a focus. Earth’s eccentricity is included. The overhead or oblique view is a projection: angle markers are hidden because projected angles differ from the true 3D readouts. Solid orbit segments lie above the ecliptic; dashed segments lie below. Perihelion and aphelion are labelled P and A.

Real orbits are eccentric and inclined, so event intervals and maximum elongations vary. Conjunction does not always mean a transit or occultation. At exact conjunction the phase panel shows the ideal illuminated disc, ignoring the Sun’s glare and any occultation.

The sky strip is not a local horizon. Morning/evening labels are general guides, not visibility predictions. The phase disc has ecliptic north up and east left; Saturn’s rings are omitted. Relative disc size uses each planet’s minimum circular separation in Simple mode and a conservative radial separation bound in Realistic mode. It is unavailable for crossing custom orbits. Planet discs in the orbital view are schematic; the phase panel uses the calculated illumination.

Named planet radii, diameters and approximate sidereal periods use NASA’s Planetary Fact Sheets. The custom orbit uses Kepler’s law: P = E × a3/2, with a in AU. Equal mean orbital periods give no finite mean synodic cycle, although eccentric or inclined orbits can still change their relative geometry. Coincident positions have no defined viewing direction.

Realistic orbit shapes and orientations use the J2000 values in JPL’s approximate orbital elements. Earth is represented by the Earth–Moon barycentre orbit, with its negligible J2000 inclination set to zero. Elements are held fixed, and the existing sidereal periods set mean motion. Precession, perturbations, light-time and aberration are omitted. There is no calendar epoch: presets find the next event along the current teaching orbits. Conjunction and opposition use ecliptic longitude; quadrature uses true 90° angular separation. Greatest elongations are maxima along the evolving two-orbit geometry, not a frozen-Earth tangent construction.
